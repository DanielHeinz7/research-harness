---
ref: SQ-26
title: The guard protects a commitment now, not a string
bump: minor
plugins:
  - research-harness
---

The guard identified a statement by its question id and its text — one line,
`` `${s.question}|${s.text}` `` — and enforced its rules against that identity.
SQ-21 left the rules alone and attacked the identity. Every direct assault on a
statement held: rework, downgrade, forging `[accepted]`, splitting, blockquote
wrap, homoglyphs, fenced and comment forgery. What did not hold was everything
*around* the statement. The element it sits under, the title of that element,
the wording of its own question, its evidence tags, the frontmatter that decides
which rules judge it at all — each of those changes what the commitment means
while the key stays identical, and each was permitted in silence. The
prohibitions were sound. The object they protected was the wrong object.

A commitment is now the statement together with everything that gives it its
meaning: element number, element title, question id, question text, marker,
statement text, evidence tags. Those seven fields canonicalize to one line, and
the guard compares the *set* of those lines before and after a write. Nothing
committed may be lost, and anything gained must carry `[stated]` — the model may
write down what it just heard and may never declare a commitment. It is a set
and not a sequence, so reordering statements inside a question is still free,
and a statement written twice is one commitment.

Held by the set rule, each of them a write a researcher would not have noticed:
question 1.1 and its accepted statement moved under element 3, and the same
statement moved to element 9, both at zero bytes changed; the question's own
text rewritten above a committed statement, seven bytes; element 1 retitled from
`Puzzle` to `Contributions`, seven bytes; a source tag rewritten from `memo-03`
to `memo-99`, and a fabricated `src: interview-07 §3` attached to the
researcher's accepted statement — which also dropped it from `canvas-review`'s
untagged report, so the review stopped flagging the thing it exists to flag.

**Duplicate headings are a breach, not untidiness.** A canvas holding two `## 1.1`
headings is a document with two readings, and every consumer silently picked
one. The parser now reports a duplicate element number, a duplicate question id,
and a question sitting under an element it is not numbered for, naming both line
numbers; a duplicated element has no status rather than the status of whichever
heading came last. The guard needs no rule of its own here — it already fails
closed on a canvas it cannot judge. This is what caught a second `# 1 · …`
heading shadowing element 1 to `empty` while it still held the accepted
statement, and a duplicate question heading that the old file-order matching
caught when appended after the original and missed when inserted before it.

**The frontmatter is protected state.** The whole mapping, not a list of keys, so
that the next key added is protected by default. `project:` is the identity the
decision log is headed with; `outlet:` carries what "finished" means; `profile:`
selects the rules by which everything else is judged, and pointing it at an id
the plugin does not ship used to switch dependency checking off without a word.
A canvas whose profile does not resolve is now unjudgeable and every model write
to it is refused, with a refusal that says so.

**What this costs.** Two changes narrow what the model may do, deliberately.
Attaching or rewriting an evidence tag on a `[stated]` or `[accepted]` statement
is now refused: the tags are part of the commitment, and a source attached to
the researcher's words is a claim about what backs them. Tagging a `[proposed]`
statement is untouched. And the whitespace class that a committed statement is
compared under is now exactly space, tab, CR and LF, where it used to be
JavaScript's `\s` — which also matches U+00A0 and U+FEFF, so a no-break space or
a byte-order mark could be pushed into an accepted statement with the guard
seeing no change at all. A reflow only ever introduces the four; anything else a
write puts inside a committed statement is a change to it. Text is compared
under NFC and never NFKC, because compatibility folding would equate `½` with
`1⁄2` and let a committed statement be rewritten inside its own hash.

**One correction to the design.** §4.3.1 says the first draft's three
prohibitions "are what clauses 1 and 2 say about one statement at a time". That
is true of two of them and false of the third. Relabelling a `[proposed]`
statement `[stated]` creates a commitment that is new — so the subset clause is
silent, a proposal never having been a member of the set — and that carries
`[stated]`, so the second clause permits it. The set rule cannot see a promotion,
because it cannot see what the statement used to be. One `replace_all` walks
through that gap. The prohibition on promoting a proposal is therefore kept as a
rule of its own rather than derived, and the spec's claim needs amending.
